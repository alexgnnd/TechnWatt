Most buying guides start with a list of models. That is the mistake: the right car depends on your use, not on this month's rankings. Here is the method we apply, in order. By the end, usually only two or three models are left to compare.

## 1. Where will you charge?

This is the question that eliminates the most options, and the only one to ask first.

**You have a private space** — garage, lock-up, driveway. Everything becomes simple: you charge overnight, range matters far less than you think, and a 50 kWh battery is plenty.

**You live in a shared building** — check whether an installation is possible before anything else. The right to a charge point exists, but implementation takes months.

**You only have the street** — electric is still possible, but the calculation changes entirely: you need strong rapid charging and chargers near home or work. Check your neighbourhood's charging map before looking at a single car.

## 2. How many long trips a year?

Not "do you go on holiday", but how often. Twice a year, an urban model is enough: you will make two extra stops, and that is two days out of three hundred and sixty-five. Twice a month, rapid charging and motorway range become the main criterion.

## 3. What range is actually useful?

The WLTP figure is a laboratory standard, not a promise. On the motorway at 130 km/h in winter, expect **60 to 70% of the claimed range**. A car quoted at 400 km will therefore cover 250 to 280 real kilometres between stops in the worst conditions.

Do the maths from your longest regular trip, not from the theoretical record.

## 4. Charging speed matters more than battery size

This is the most overlooked criterion and one of the most decisive. Between two cars with the same range, the one accepting 150 kW rather than 80 kW saves you twenty minutes at every stop. On a long run, that changes the day.

Look at DC charging power, and be wary of headline peaks: what counts is the 10 to 80% time.

## 5. Buy or lease?

Long-term leasing dominates the electric market for a specific reason: it transfers residual-value risk to the lessor, and that value remains the great unknown. If you keep your cars for ten years, buying makes sense again. If you change every three years, leasing is almost always wiser.

Either way, look at the included mileage and the cost per extra kilometre before the monthly payment.

## 6. Which incentives, and on what date?

Support schemes change often, and they generally apply on the **delivery date**, not the signature date. A six-month lead time can push a case into the following year and wipe out the advantage. Ask the seller, in writing.

## In summary

If you have answered the six questions, your specification is written: a budget, a real minimum range, a floor charging power, a financing method. All that remains is to find the models that tick those boxes — and only then does a comparison become useful.
