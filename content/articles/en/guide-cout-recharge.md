"Charging costs nothing." "Charging is as expensive as petrol." Both statements circulate, and both are accurate — they simply are not talking about the same place. Here is how to do the maths yourself, once and for all.

## The formula, and it fits on one line

**Cost of a full charge = battery capacity (kWh) × price per kWh (€)**

That is all. A 60 kWh battery charged at €0.20/kWh costs €12. The same at €0.60/kWh costs €36. The whole question therefore lies in the price per kWh, which varies by a factor of four to eight depending on where you are.

For cost per kilometre, divide by the real range. A car using 18 kWh/100 km charged at €0.20 works out at **€3.60 per 100 km**. The same car charged at €0.60 costs €10.80.

## The three worlds of charging

**At home, off-peak.** This is the lowest rate by far. With an off-peak tariff and charging scheduled overnight, you are at the bottom of the range. This is the reference scenario: the vast majority of charges happen here.

**On a public slow or medium charger.** Street, car parks, supermarkets. Rates are intermediate, sometimes free at certain retailers, and often billed by time as well — watch for parking fees that kick in after a grace period.

**On a motorway rapid charger.** This is the highest rate, two to three times the home price. It is also the one everyone quotes in conversation, even though it covers a minority of charges. On these chargers, a monthly subscription with an operator pays for itself after a few sessions a month.

## The time-billing trap

Some chargers bill per kWh, others per minute, others both. Per-minute billing heavily penalises cars that charge slowly: on a rapid charger, a car peaking at 50 kW pays for the same time as a 150 kW car for three times less energy.

One more reason to check a vehicle's accepted charging power before buying it.

## What to compare against combustion

For an honest comparison, use your own fuel consumption and your own annual mileage, not averages. And compare like with like: cost per 100 km, not the price of a fill-up — a battery and a fuel tank do not hold the same amount.

Add servicing on both sides too. This is where electric pulls away most consistently: no oil changes, no belt, no clutch, and pads that last longer thanks to regenerative braking.

## In summary

The cost of charging is not a value, it is a range. If you charge at home overnight, electric is clearly cheaper to run than combustion. If you depend exclusively on motorway rapid chargers, the gap narrows sharply, sometimes to nothing. Which is precisely why the first question to ask before buying is not range, but where you will charge.
