On 13 November, Tesla invited a selection of American customers to order the Cybercab for personal use. That is a turning point: until now, the autonomous two-seater was reserved for robotaxi fleets. The headline price, $29,990 excluding tax, is worth a look. So is everything that comes after it.

## A subscription without which nothing moves

Ordering requires a non-refundable $1,000 deposit. Then an **Autonomy** subscription at $299 a month, with the first month free. In its terms dated 12 November, Tesla states explicitly that the Cybercab cannot operate without an active subscription.

In other words: buying it does not grant the right to drive. It grants the right to subscribe. Over three years, the subscription adds close to $10,000, roughly a third of the vehicle's price.

## No steering wheel, no pedals, no way out

The Cybercab is a two-seater with no steering wheel and no pedals. There is no manual driving mode: if the software does not take over, nobody else will. That is the logical consequence of a vehicle designed from the start for autonomous driving — and the reason the subscription is technically indispensable, not merely commercially.

The terms also impose driving zones: Tesla decides where the car may go. And owners cannot use it to earn money, which closes the door on offsetting the subscription by renting the vehicle out.

## What is under the bodywork

The Cybercab is built at the Austin Gigafactory in Texas. It uses a single 163 kW motor, or 219 hp, and a 47.6 kWh battery for around 472 km of range. First deliveries to private buyers are announced for December, in Austin.

## And in Europe?

None of this concerns the European market yet. Type-approval conditions for a vehicle without a steering wheel are considerably stricter here, and Tesla's Robotaxi service currently operates only in the United States. The question for us lies elsewhere: is this business model — low purchase price, mandatory subscription — the one that will reach conventional cars next?

## Our verdict

The Cybercab raises a question the car industry had so far avoided: what is left of ownership when the manufacturer decides whether, where and on what terms the vehicle may be driven? At $29,990, Tesla is not selling a cheaper car — it is selling a subscription, with a car around it. That may well be the future. It is not the same thing as buying a car.
