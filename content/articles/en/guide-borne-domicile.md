Charging at home is the scenario that makes electric motoring cheap. Provided the installation goes well. In the quotes we see, the gap between two neighbouring houses can be threefold — and almost never because of the charger itself.

## Which power rating?

This is the simplest decision, and the one most often got wrong.

**7.4 kW single-phase** covers virtually all domestic needs. An eight-hour night delivers close to 60 kWh, more than a full battery for most models. It is the default choice, and the right one nine times out of ten.

**11 or 22 kW three-phase** only makes sense if two conditions are met: your property actually has a three-phase supply, **and** your car accepts that power on AC. Many models top out at 11 kW, some at 7.4. Paying for a 22 kW charger the car cannot exploit is money gone.

Check that line in the vehicle's spec sheet before ordering anything.

## What really moves the quote

1. **The distance between the consumer unit and the charger location.** The first cost item. Ten metres in an attached garage or forty metres to the end of the garden is not the same trench.
2. **The state of the consumer unit.** A full board means adding a dedicated circuit, sometimes a sub-board.
3. **Your supply rating.** A 7.4 kW charger takes a large share of a 9 kVA supply. Upgrading to 12 kVA is sometimes necessary, with an extra monthly cost.
4. **Cable routing.** Chasing, trunking or trenching: the price is nothing alike.
5. **Shared buildings.** The right to a charge point exists, but the managing agent must be notified and a waiting period observed. Expect several months.
6. **Residual current protection.** Some chargers include it, others require an additional device at the board.

## The IRVE installer is not optional

Above 3.7 kW, installation must be carried out by an IRVE-qualified professional. This is not mere paperwork: it conditions the warranty, your insurance cover in case of an incident, and access to any available support schemes.

A charger bought on sale and fitted by an unqualified electrician exposes you on all three counts at once.

## The method that avoids nasty surprises

Get the installation quoted **before** buying the charger. Two quotes minimum, with an on-site technical visit — a quote given over the phone is worthless. Ask for the quote to itemise hardware, labour and any electrical work separately.

Once the fitting is quoted and the charger model settled, you can shop for hardware at the best price: [Type 2 home chargers](https://www.amazon.fr/s?k=borne+de+recharge+type+2+7%2C4+kW) and [charging cables](https://www.amazon.fr/s?k=cable+de+recharge+type+2+voiture+electrique) fall sharply during promotional periods. In that order, the discount is a bonus. In the other, it is a source of trouble.

## What about a reinforced socket?

It is the intermediate solution: around 3.2 kW, twice a standard domestic socket, for far less than a wallbox. It suits a low-mileage driver with a modest battery and a private parking space. Beyond that, charging time becomes a constraint. It is not a substitute for a charger, it is a first step.
